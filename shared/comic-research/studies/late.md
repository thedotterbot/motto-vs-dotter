## Cross-strip findings

- A private success metric often explains behavior that looks like failure: insults without customers, rejection that secretly requests attention, and paperwork in place of learning.
- Quiet reaction panels are causal beats, not empty space. They establish that another character has understood the proposal before refusing it.
- Fantasy often preserves the physical action while changing scale or agency: a wave becomes a disaster, a morning becomes automation, a ball becomes a threat, and a bicycle becomes an enemy.
- The best ending is not always a put-down. Friendship supplies solace after visual pressure, and a spatially open coda can deliver emotional release.
- Formal language is funniest when its governing assumptions visibly fail: legal protections without power, friendship without voluntary choice, or credentials without evidence.
- A visual montage can accumulate evidence that a final short sentence misinterprets. The interpretation should belong to the character rather than being treated as the strip’s conclusion.

## CH-L-001: Unwanted free samples

Source: [It’s a Magical World — Printed page 60, top](https://publishingassets.andrewsmcmeel.com/productimages/9780836221367_insidepage_1.jpg)
Type: daily. Original publication date: unknown. Observed: 2026-10-23.

**Synopsis:** Calvin sells harsh appearance assessments, yet his actual satisfaction comes from supplying them without customers.
**Panel count/layout:** 3 panels, horizontal; stationary sidewalk stand
**Setup:** A sign introduces the odd service before Hobbes asks whether anyone is buying.
**Character motive or belief:** Calvin wants permission to insult people, more than he wants to run a viable business.
**Causal mechanism:** The final boast changes apparent commercial failure into success under a concealed personal objective.
**Words vs images:** The small booth and earnest salesman posture make an antisocial impulse look like ordinary enterprise. The dialogue exposes the real reward.
**Pacing:** Two restrained exchanges precede Calvin’s delighted expression; the background stays still so the reinterpretation carries the beat.
**Theme:** Commerce; self-deception; social aggression.
**Payoff or reaction:** Hobbes discovers that lack of sales has not reduced Calvin’s output.
**Transferable lesson:** Give a character two competing success metrics. Let a practical observer judge the visible one, then reveal the private metric that explains the behavior.
**Avoid copying:** Do not reuse the appearance-rating stand, pricing, or free-sample reversal.
