## What works and why (the content, not the funnel)

- **Output first.** The reel opens on finished clips,
  not on explanation. The viewer sees the prize before
  anyone says how it was made. Output-first hooks beat
  process-first hooks: desire precedes instruction.
- **Input-to-output demonstration.** A short prompt goes
  in, varied clips come out. The transformation IS the
  content. No claim is made that the demo does not show.
- **Comparison panels prove consistency.** Still-style
  panels showing a consistent visual system across
  outputs. That is the difference between "one lucky
  output" and "a system that works." Repeatability shown,
  not claimed.
- **Visual novelty holds attention.** Surreal imagery
  (camera-headed figure, pocket watch with butterflies)
  is unusual enough to stop the scroll on its own. The
  strangeness is the hook.
- **Director framing.** Large on-screen text emphasizes
  creator direction over manual frame-by-frame work. The
  creator is positioned as director, not button-pusher.
  That framing flatters the viewer (you direct; the tool
  executes) and matches how our pipeline actually works
  (we direct, code renders).
- **Three-mode variety.** Fast cuts rotate between
  generated clips, screen recording, and talking head.
  The mode change itself is pacing — the viewer never
  sits in one register long enough to drift.
- **Bold captions carry it muted.** Large on-screen text
  means the message survives with sound off.

## What does NOT transfer to us

- **The workflow is not ours.** These are prompt-to-video
  generated clips from a tool we do not use. Our pipeline
  is code-composed (HyperFrames), not prompt-to-video.
  The workflow asset is theirs; the demo grammar is
  what transfers, not the tool.
- **Proof is demo-only.** No metrics, no before/after
  measurements, no testimonials. The clips are the
  evidence — and sampled frames cannot show whether
  they are typical outputs or cherry-picked best ones.
  Survivorship presentation: one prompt shown, failed
  generations and prompt tuning hidden.
- **Talking-head delivery is a different format.** Our
  bet is character-led, not human-to-camera. The
  speaker segment works here; it does not transfer to a
  channel whose face is a plush puppet.
- **"Short prompt → output" hides iteration.** The reel
  shows the clean version: prompt in, clips out. Real
  production is prompt, fail, retune, select. Our reels
  are honest about iteration (disasters included) — that
  honesty is a differentiator, not a weakness.

## What we apply

1. **Output-first hook.** Future reels can open on the
   finished thing — the disaster, the gag, the result —
   before explaining how it was made. Prize first,
   process second.
2. **Comparison panels as proof of a system.** When we
   claim a system works, show multiple outputs side by
   side. Our reels 01/02/04 as a row = proof of a system,
   not one lucky video. Panels, not promises.
3. **Director framing as content.** Motto as director,
   not button-pusher. Scripts can name direction
   decisions (why this framing, why this cut) as content
   — the decisions are the expertise.
4. **Three-mode variety.** Clips / screen / talking-head
   rotation is a pacing template. Our equivalent: gag /
   set / Motto-to-camera. Rotate registers, never sit in
   one.
5. **Show the input.** When we demo a workflow, show the
   actual prompt or input on screen — our real prompts,
   real code — not just the output. Input shown is what
   makes a demo checkable.
