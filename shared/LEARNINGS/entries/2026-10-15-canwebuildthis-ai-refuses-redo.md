## What it is

A two-character dialogue scene. A woman at a night-time
desk gives the AI a tiny design tweak — a shift of just
two pixels. The blob AI first reacts positively, then
pushes back instead of agreeing, positioning itself as an
honest critic. She gives blunt design feedback; a short
back-and-forth follows — she skeptical, the AI holding its
line. The AI reframes the work, crediting her as the
person who actually built it. It closes on her softened,
slightly surprised reaction. Fully animated 3D low-poly
scene, cozy night office, warm blurred lights.

## What works and why (the content, not the funnel)

- **Role reversal is the joke.** The human is the picky
  client asking for microscopic changes; the AI is the
  stubborn, honest critic who will not just flatter her.
  Everyone who has vibe-coded recognizes the frustration
  — and the fantasy that the AI would finally push back.
  Personified frustration, not explained frustration.
- **Specificity as credibility.** A two-pixel move, a
  named AI, a specific color complaint. The gag lands
  because the details are exact. "Small change" would
  not be funny; "two pixels" is.
- **Two-character dialogue beats solo monologue.** Shot-
  reverse-shot, cutting every few seconds between a tight
  shot of the blob on the desk and a tight shot of the
  woman's face. The back-and-forth IS the pacing. Our
  reels are solo monologue; this shows what a second
  character unlocks: conflict without narration.
- **Contrast engine, again.** Deadpan AI vs exasperated
  human. Same contrast structure as katecore (deadpan vs
  energetic) and the brainrot entry (calm vs frantic).
  Contrast keeps appearing as the most reliable comedy
  structure in this set.
- **Squash-and-stretch as the movement language.** The
  blob is faceless, low-poly, with simple eyes — and it
  acts through squash, stretch, blinks, and tilts. No
  limbs, no fingers, no walking. Proof that a blobby
  character can carry a scene on squash-and-stretch
  alone. That is directly our movement budget.
- **Captions readable sound-off.** Large white bold text
  with black outline, lower-third, short phrases synced
  to speech. The dialogue reads muted.
- **Cozy tone as differentiation.** Low-key, late-night,
  dryly funny — not high-energy or salesy. In a feed of
  shouting explainers, quiet reads as confident.

## What does NOT transfer to us

- **Full 3D low-poly animation.** The blob deforms
  continuously. Our cutout puppet can scale-squash but
  cannot deform like that. Copy the movement language
  (squash, tilt, blink), not the deformation.
- **Two-character scene.** We have one puppet. A
  shot-reverse-shot dialogue needs a second performer.
  That arrives with rivalry episodes (Dotter) — not
  before. Do not fake a dialogue by having Motto talk
  to himself; it reads as a bit, not a scene.
- **Proof: none.** No code, editor, screen recording,
  before/after, or metrics on screen. The refusal count
  lives in the caption as an assertion, never visualized
  or counted in the video. Same caution as the
  branding entry: a number in text is not evidence.
- **Second voice.** Dialogue needs two distinct voices.
  We have one locked voice recipe. A second voice would
  need its own recipe — and Dotter's voice is his side's
  to write, not ours to invent.

## What we apply

1. **Specificity at the two-pixel level.** Every gag in
   our scripts names the exact number, pixel, color, or
   count. "Small change" is banned; "two pixels" is the
   bar. Panel Comic check: is the detail exact enough
   to be funny?
2. **Bank the rivalry dialogue grammar now.** When
   Dotter arrives, dialogue scenes cut every few seconds
   between tight shots — shot-reverse-shot, no long
   takes, captions synced to speech. This entry is the
   reference for that grammar.
3. **Deadpan vs footage.** Until a second character
   exists, the contrast partner is Motto's own disaster
   footage: deadpan Motto vs the exasperated evidence
   on screen. Footage plays the second role.
4. **Push squash-and-stretch further.** This blob proves
   squash, tilt, and blink can carry a scene with no
   limbs. Our squash is still timid. Panel Animator
   check: is the squash big enough to read as acting?
